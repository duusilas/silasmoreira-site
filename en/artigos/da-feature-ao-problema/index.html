The problem before the feature.

Between a request and a product decision there is discovery work: getting to the problem, the hypothesis and a useful test.

Silas Moreira8 Nov 20266 min read

Requests like "more filters" or "a dashboard" often arrive already shaped as a solution. That's understandable: it's easier to imagine a solution than to describe a difficulty.

"We need a new filter."

Product work starts when we create space between the request and the decision to build. Who needs what? What's stopping them? What evidence exists? What would change if the problem were solved?

One request can hide different problems.

RequestMore filters.
ProblemThe buyer struggles to tell eligible vehicles apart.
HypothesisConsistent, comparable attributes help decide.
TestA reorganised list. The filter is just one of the solutions to compare.
RequestAn audience dashboard.
ProblemKnowing whether an audience is up to date and ready to activate.
HypothesisShowing definition, freshness and eligibility makes it possible to decide the next step.
TestA simple view, validated with sales and operations.

Six questions to give context to the decision.

They separate what we already know from what we still need to learn. They don't require a long document, just a concrete enough conversation.

  1. Who needs this?

    Describe a person and a concrete situation. "The users" is too generic.

  2. What difficulty exists today?

    Separate the request from the real need. An export can hide the difficulty of sharing information.

  3. What evidence do we have?

    Conversations, observations, support requests, usage data. Tell fact apart from hypothesis.

  4. What has already been tried?

    Understand how the person solves the problem today, before building something new.

  5. How will we know it helped?

    Define an observable signal: completing a task, reducing errors, needing less help. And set a date to review.

  6. What is the smallest useful test?

    Choose the cheapest test that lets you learn (prototype, guided conversation, manual experiment) and what would change the decision.

The result is a more explainable decision.

The team can build, try an alternative, gather more evidence or postpone. What matters is being able to explain the link between need, evidence and choice.
